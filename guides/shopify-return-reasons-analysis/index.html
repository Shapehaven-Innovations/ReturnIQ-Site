How to analyze Shopify return reasons

Last updated October 8, 2026

To find out why products come back, group your returns by product and by return reason, then look for the combinations that repeat. A single store-wide return rate cannot show you that.

Why return rate alone is not enough

A return rate tells you that sales reversed. It does not tell you that the sizing ran small, that the photos made a color look different in person, or that a handful of customers send back a large share of what they buy. Those are different problems with different fixes, and they all look identical in one percentage.

Instead of comparing your rate with a generic average, compare it with your own history, split by product, variant and reason. Averages vary widely by category, so a benchmark from another kind of store is rarely useful.

What Shopify now gives you

Shopify's changelog entry from January 16, 2026 describes suggested return reasons that are specific to the product being returned, drawn from Shopify's Standard Product Taxonomy, so the options depend on the product's category. They are available automatically, with no setup. Shopify's own summary is that better return reasons mean better data.

Better reasons help, but they are still individual records. You still need to group them and compare them across products to see a pattern.

A five-step way to analyze return reasons

1. Collect the right fields

For every return you want: the product, the variant (size, color), the return reason, the date, and the customer. Shopify's admin and exports give you most of this; a spreadsheet is enough to start.

2. Group by product and by reason

Count returns for each product and reason pair. Sort from the largest group down. The top of that list is where to look first, because it combines volume with a specific cause.

3. Look for clusters

The patterns worth acting on tend to look like one of these:

  • Same product, same reason. For example, one dress mostly returned as "too small". That points at the size chart or the cut, not at your customers.
  • Same variant. One color or size returns far more than its siblings, which often means a photo, listing or quality issue with just that variant.
  • Same reason across a category. The same complaint across many similar products suggests a category-wide problem such as sizing guidance.

4. Separate product problems from customer patterns

If returns for a product are spread across many different customers, treat it as a product or listing problem. If a few customers account for a large share of returns, treat it as a customer pattern and review your policy and how you flag repeat returners.

5. Fix one cluster, then measure it the same way

Pick the biggest cluster, change one thing (the size chart, the photos, the description, the packaging), and re-run the same grouping after enough orders have come in to compare. If the group shrinks, the diagnosis was right.

Common traps

  • Acting on tiny samples. Two returns do not make a pattern. Wait until a group is large enough that one customer cannot explain it.
  • Vague reasons. If most returns are "other" or "changed my mind", the reason data is too coarse to guide a fix. Better reason options at checkout are worth improving first.
  • Seasonality. Compare periods that are comparable, such as peak season against peak season.
  • Mixing exchanges and refunds. They usually mean different things; keep them separate in your counts.
